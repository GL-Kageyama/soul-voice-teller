# design — the design document

> Skills: `/plot-design` (base) → `/narration-design` · `/character-forge` · `/character-in-action` · `/worldbuild` · `/world-iceberg` (elaboration) · Input: premise + demo persona

## Required design (what should be fixed before fast-draft writes)

### Logline
A keeper who fears the sea keeps a light and waits for the one guest who could only come by sea.

### Narration
- point of view: third person, limited (internal focalization on the keeper)
- tense: past
- narrator: a watcher at a distance; the keeper's interior is never explained, only reached through what he does and leaves undone

**Type of point of view (why)** — third person, limited to the keeper, and the limit is kept so strictly that the interior never opens. The reader stands beside the keeper and sees only his hands and the water. An omniscient view would name the feeling and break the restraint; a first person would force the keeper to speak a "why" he does not have. Limited-third holds the reader near the body and refuses the mind.

**Reliability** — reliable. The narrator withholds nothing that is seen and adds nothing that is not. What it withholds is the interior, and that withholding is the design, not a lie.

**Distance** — near the body, far from the mind. The reader watches the keeper the way the keeper watches the water.

**Tense** — past. The past is a distance already traveled; the waiting reads as something done and still doing.

**Nesting** — none. No frame, no letter, no diary. To nest the story would give the waiting a "teller" and an explanation, and the story refuses explanation.

**Speech style** — almost no speech. The keeper does not speak; the supply boat does not arrive on the page. Narration is carried by description (camera) with free indirect motion at one remove — a line that rides an action, never an interior named. Where the point of view is kept and where it might break: it is kept in all three scenes; the only near-break is free indirect at one remove, and even that stays on the surface.

### Direction of prose style
Short sentences. Breathe at the period. Avoid adjectives; write with nouns and verbs. Feeling through action and silence, never named. The anchor image — tea poured for a guest who never comes, going cold — is woven once, in the middle, and never repeated in words.

### Scene table
| # | scene | purpose (what it achieves) | who | what changes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A storm night; he tends the lamp and goes down to the water's edge | Set the fear and the pull of the sea side by side | the keeper | He confirms again: nothing comes tonight. He climbs back. |
| 2 | A still noon; he pours tea for two | Show the waiting as an action | the keeper | The second cup cools. He drinks his own, empties the other. |
| 3 | Dusk; he smooths the sand at the waterline and lights the lamp | Close the waiting in an action, not a resolution | the keeper | Nothing changes; only the time passes. |

**How the character's setting functions in each scene (character-in-action)**:

- Scene 1 — *because* he fears the sea, he must go down to it; *because* he waits, he watches the light out on the water until it turns away and is gone. The wound is not spoken; it is behaved in the way he walks to the water's edge and does not call out. Linkage: the fear and the pull live in one action.
- Scene 2 — *because* the guest never comes, the tea is poured anyway; the second cup is the waiting made into a daily act. The past (the one who is gone) is not told; it is behaved in the second cup, set down without thought, in the place that stays empty. Linkage: the action is the man.
- Scene 3 — *because* the guest might come at dusk, the sand must be smooth; *because* the sea took someone, he works at the waterline, where the tide reaches. He smooths his own morning footprints away from the water, back toward the rocks — not to erase himself but to leave the sand clear. Linkage: the smoothing is the waiting.

**Order of disclosure (staged)** — the wound is never disclosed; it is held. What is disclosed, by distribution, is the shape of the waiting: scene 1 shows the fear and the pull; scene 2 shows the waiting as a daily act; scene 3 shows the waiting as a thing that will not end. The backstory (who was lost) is withheld through all three scenes and left as negative space.

### Reader image & promise
For a reader who has waited. Grab at the storm (scene 1), hold at the tea (scene 2), release at dusk (scene 3) — leaving the reader the space to feel their own waiting. The promise is a quiet displacement, and it is kept by not closing.

## Optional preparation

### Character
The keeper. Middle-aged. He has kept the light by himself for years.

- **desire · goal**: that the guest come — the one who could only come by sea. A wish he does not speak, and performs anyway, every day, in the second cup.
- **motive**: if the guest comes, the waiting ends. To stop waiting would be to let go of the one the sea took. So the guest must keep being possible.
- **lack · wound**: someone was lost to the sea (`?`). It is never said who, or how. The wound is held, not spoken; it lives in the second cup, in the smoothing of the sand, in the light he keeps burning.
- **belief · values**: the light must not go out. Keeping it is the one thing that is not waiting — it is a thing done, every dusk, without fail.
- **voice**: he almost never speaks. What words he has are few, practical, to himself — "no one tonight." Vocabulary of the routine: lamp, wick, oil, glass, door, tide, sand. No names of feeling.
- **change arc**: he does not change. The arc is a non-arc — the waiting is not resolved, only carried. What changes is that the reader stops expecting it to resolve and begins to feel its weight.
- **the gap between surface and interior**: he looks like a man doing his work — competent, routine, calm. The interior is a fear and a hope that share one place. The surface never shows it; the actions (the second cup, the smoothed sand) are the only door to it.

**The inner conflict (narrowed to one)**: he fears the sea, and he needs the sea. The same water that took what he lost is the only road by which it could return. He cannot turn from it, and he cannot trust it. This one collision is the engine; there is no second conflict.

**How the character ties to the theme**: the keeper is the theme's living argument. The question "why does a person keep waiting for something that never comes?" is not answered with a reason; it is answered with a man who is still waiting.

### World
- **setting · place**: a lighthouse on a rock, set off the coast. The rock is small enough to walk across; the water is on all sides. The sea is not background — it is the horizon of every room.
- **era**: not fixed to a named year (`?`). The details (an oil lamp, a hand-cleaned lens) sit before electricity reaches such rocks; the era is implied by the lamp, not stated.
- **geography · nature**: the sea is the whole weather. Storms come off it; the flat noon comes off it; the dusk comes off it. There is no inland. The sound of the water never stops.
- **social structure · economy**: none to speak of. The keeper is by himself; a supply boat comes by sea at intervals, and it is not the guest he waits for. The waiting is not social — it is against the sea itself.
- **culture · custom · religion · myth**: a single private custom — the second cup. It is the keeper's own rite, unshared, unwitnessed, and it is the story's only ceremony.
- **history · chronology**: the keeper's past is the one event, and it is not written. The chronology of the story is three days — or three pieces of one endless day; the design leaves this loose.
- **technology**: the lamp. Oil, a wick, a lens he cleans each day. The light is the technology and it is also the promise: it must not go out.
- **the supernatural · magic**: none. The sea is ordinary water, and that is what makes it terrible — behind it is nothing but distance and depth.
- **language · names**: no one is named. The keeper is "the keeper", the guest is "the guest", the lost one is never named at all. The refusal of names is part of the restraint.
- **the world's promise**: a world of one man and the sea, where nothing supernatural will come — only weather, and time, and the light.

**Internal consistency**: the rules do not break. The sea cannot be crossed except by boat; the guest, if any, can only come by sea; therefore the keeper, who fears the sea, must keep his eyes on the water. Every scene obeys this. Nothing arrives that could not arrive by water.

**Setting ⇔ story**: the sea is not where the story happens; the sea *is* the conflict. The keeper's fear and his hope are both the sea. The setting is what makes the story possible — a man on a rock, with only one road in, waiting on a road he cannot trust.

**Conveying the setting**: the sea is shown, not explained — through the waves at the foot of the tower, the flat noon, the tide washing the smoothed sand. The setting enters as weather and light, never as description for its own sake.

**The unwritten world (world-iceberg) — in order of touching probability**:

1. The supply boat (high) — what it brings, how often, why it is not the guest. One line: it comes by sea on a schedule the story never states; it delivers oil and food; it is not him.
2. The lamp's routine (high) — one paragraph, backstage: he cleans the lens each day, trims the wick, checks the oil at dusk. Material the story touches in every scene but never explains.
3. The interior of the tower (high) — the stairs, the room with the lens, the small room below with the table and two chairs. Enough spatial sense that the tea scene has its place.
4. The lost one (high as a felt presence, low as a fact) — deliberately *not* documented. The one thing the story must not verbalize. Kept in mind only as a felt absence.
5. The mainland and its distance (medium) — how far, what town, whether anyone waits for the keeper. One line: too far to see from the rock, present only as the place the supply boat comes from.
6. The seasons and the years (medium) — whether this is one year or many. Undecided (`?`); the story reads as one unending season, and the looseness is kept.
7. The keeper's name (low, deliberate) — not documented. The refusal of the name is the design, not a gap.

**Hierarchy of material**: what the reader sees (the sea, the lamp, the tea, the sand) vs. what only the writer holds (the lost one, the supply schedule, the distance to the mainland). The reader's material is all the story shows; the backstage material is held so it does not contradict when touched. Unit: a proper name or a fact is one line; the lamp's routine and the tower's interior are one paragraph each; the rock and the water's reach are a figure held in mind.

**The freedom not to document**: the lost one is not written down, on purpose. To write "who was lost" would be to fill the negative space the whole story leaves open. The empty space is preserved by not verbalizing it.

### Foreshadowing
- Who the guest is is never told (`?`).
- The second cup, laid in scene 2, is not recovered in any scene. It is a set-aside, an open gesture — not a planted clue with a payoff. (Recorded in the bible as unrecovered, kept open.)
